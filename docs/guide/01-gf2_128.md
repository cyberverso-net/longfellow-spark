# Chapter 1: the binary field GF(2^128)

Package `GF2_128` (`src/gf2_128.ads`, `src/gf2_128.adb`). Stage 1 of phase A, complete. Decisions: [ADR 0004](../adr/0004-gf2-128-element-representation.md) to [ADR 0010](../adr/0010-assertion-enabled-test-project.md). Specification: draft-google-cfrg-libzk-03, Section 2.2.2 (field definition), Section 3.4.3 (bytes to coefficients), Section 7.2.1 (serialisation).

## 1. The mathematics

### 1.1 The field

GF(2) is the field with two elements, 0 and 1, where addition is xor and multiplication is and. Polynomials with coefficients in GF(2) are added coefficient-wise (xor) and multiplied as usual, except that there are no carries: the product of x^i and x^j is x^(i+j), and like terms cancel in pairs.

Pick an irreducible polynomial Q(x) of degree 128 over GF(2). The polynomials of degree below 128, with addition as above and multiplication followed by reduction modulo Q(x), form a field with 2^128 elements: GF(2^128). "Reduction" means replacing the product, which may have degree up to 254, by its remainder on division by Q(x). Every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.

The draft fixes

    Q(x) = x^128 + x^7 + x^2 + x + 1

(Section 2.2.2). This is a pentanomial of low weight, so reduction is cheap: since Q(x) = 0 in the field,

    x^128 = x^7 + x^2 + x + 1

and any term of degree 128 or more can be rewritten downwards by substituting this identity. The right-hand side, as a bit pattern, is 2#1000_0111# = 16#87#, the constant `Reduction_Low` of the package. The same polynomial is used by GHASH in AES-GCM, but GCM stores the coefficients in reflected bit order, so GCM test vectors denote different elements under this chapter's convention and are not used ([ADR 0004](../adr/0004-gf2-128-element-representation.md)).

The draft also states that x itself generates the multiplicative group of the field: the 2^128 - 1 non-zero elements are exactly the powers x^0, x^1, ..., x^(2^128 - 2). The sumcheck protocol relies on the field being large (soundness error is proportional to the circuit degree divided by the field size, by the Schwartz-Zippel lemma); characteristic 2 is what makes Boolean circuits cheap to arithmetise.

### 1.2 Representation

An element is a polynomial of degree below 128, hence 128 coefficients, hence 128 bits: bit i is the coefficient of x^i. The package stores them in two 64-bit limbs, `Limbs (0)` for bits 0 to 63 and `Limbs (1)` for bits 64 to 127. This is bit-for-bit the `u128` of the upstream Rust reference. Written as a 128-bit integer in hexadecimal (32 digits, most significant first), the element x is `...0002`, x^7 + x^2 + x + 1 is `...0087`, and x^127 is `8000...0000`.

### 1.3 Addition

Addition is xor of coefficients, hence xor of limbs. It needs no reduction (the degree does not grow), it does not depend on Q(x), and every element is its own additive inverse, so subtraction is addition.

### 1.4 Multiplication by x

Multiplying by x shifts every coefficient up by one position. If the coefficient of x^127 was 1, the shift produces x^128, which is reduced by the identity above: xor 16#87# into the low limb. The outgoing bit is called the carry.

Worked example, x^127 * x. Before: `Limbs (1) = 16#8000_0000_0000_0000#`, `Limbs (0) = 0`. The carry is bit 127, which is 1. After the shift both limbs are zero (the only set bit left through the top). Fold the carry: `Limbs (0) = 0 xor 16#87# = 16#87#`. Result: x^7 + x^2 + x + 1. This is the equality x^128 = x^7 + x^2 + x + 1 made concrete, and it is the first structural case in the vector file.

### 1.5 Multiplication

Multiplication is shift-and-add with interleaved reduction, read from the most significant bit of the second operand downwards (Horner's rule):

    R := 0
    for i from 127 down to 0:
        R := x * R                 (shift, reduce)
        if bit i of B is 1: R := R + A

After processing bit i, R equals A times the polynomial formed by bits 127 down to i of B, reduced. After bit 0, R = A * B mod Q(x). Horner's rule is nothing but the usual expansion A * B = sum over i of (bit i of B) * A * x^i, evaluated inside-out so that the degree never exceeds 128 before the next reduction. The package calls the step "x * R" `Xtime`.

### 1.6 The subfield GF(2^16) and the beta basis

The multiplicative group of GF(2^128) is cyclic of order 2^128 - 1, and 2^16 - 1 divides 2^128 - 1 (because 16 divides 128). Let

    e = (2^128 - 1) / (2^16 - 1),      g = x^e.

Then g has order 2^16 - 1, and zero together with the powers of g is the unique subfield with 2^16 elements, GF(2^16). The sixteen elements beta_i = g^i for i = 0 to 15 are linearly independent over GF(2) and form a basis of the subfield: every subfield element is a unique xor of some of them, so a 16-bit integer s denotes the element sum over k of (bit k of s) * beta_k. This is how the upstream code injects small scalars into the field.

The subfield matters for later stages. The commitment scheme encodes witness values in 16 bits instead of 128 when they lie in the subfield, which is what makes Ligero commitments to Boolean-heavy circuits compact; Fiat-Shamir samples subfield challenges from two bytes through the same basis; and the sumcheck layer evaluates at the points 0, 1 and beta_1. The draft defines g and the basis in Section 2.2.2. This chapter uses the basis for a different purpose as well: its sixteen numerical values are an external anchor for the test vectors (Section 5 below).

### 1.7 Squaring

In characteristic 2, (a + b)^2 = a^2 + b^2, because the cross term 2ab is zero. Squaring is therefore a linear map (the Frobenius map), and can be computed by spreading the bits of the input to even positions and reducing, without a general multiplication. At stage 1 squaring is implemented as a multiplication by itself; the linear fast path is deferred ([ADR 0007](../adr/0007-square-as-wrapper-over-mul.md)).

### 1.8 Inversion

By Fermat's little theorem for finite fields, every non-zero E satisfies E^(2^128 - 1) = 1, hence E^(2^128 - 2) = E^-1. The exponent 2^128 - 2 is fixed, so the inverse is a fixed sequence of squarings and multiplications, which is exactly what constant-time code wants.

Write T_k for E^(2^k - 1). Two identities make a short chain:

    T_(a+b) = (T_a)^(2^b) * T_b,   because (2^a - 1) * 2^b + (2^b - 1) = 2^(a+b) - 1

and raising to the power 2^b is b squarings. Starting from T_1 = E, the chain

    1, 2, 3, 6, 7, 14, 15, 30, 31, 62, 63, 126, 127

reaches T_127 = E^(2^127 - 1) with 12 multiplications and 126 squarings (each step T_(a+b) costs b squarings and one multiplication; the b values are 1, 1, 3, 1, 7, 1, 15, 1, 31, 1, 63, 1, summing to 126). One final squaring gives (E^(2^127 - 1))^2 = E^(2^128 - 2). This is the Itoh-Tsujii method.

Zero has no inverse. The chain applied to zero returns zero without any special case, and the package keeps that behaviour: `Inv (Zero) = Zero`, documented, matching the upstream C++ ([ADR 0008](../adr/0008-inv-fixed-chain-total-function.md)).

## 2. The Ada/SPARK implementation

This section reads the package spec and body top to bottom and explains each construct the first time it appears. Line numbers refer to the files at the commit this chapter describes.

### 2.1 The spec: types

```ada
pragma SPARK_Mode (On);

with Interfaces; use Interfaces;

package GF2_128 is
   type Limb_Index is range 0 .. 1;
   type Limb_Array is array (Limb_Index) of Unsigned_64;
   type Element is record
      Limbs : Limb_Array;
   end record;
```

`pragma SPARK_Mode (On)` tells GNATprove that this unit is to be analysed: everything in it must be in the SPARK subset, and every subprogram gets verification conditions. `Interfaces` is the standard package that provides `Unsigned_64`, a *modular type*: arithmetic wraps around 2^64 instead of raising an overflow, and the bit operations `and`, `or`, `xor`, `Shift_Left`, `Shift_Right` are defined on it. Modular types are the right tool for limbs: a shift that loses bits is intended, not an error.

`Limb_Index` is an integer type with exactly two values, and `Limb_Array` is indexed by it, so an index out of range is impossible by typing: GNATprove still generates a check wherever an index is computed (`Limb_Index (I / 64)` below) and proves it. `Element` is a record with one field; the record wrapper gives the type a name of its own and lets `=` compare whole elements.

```ada
   Zero : constant Element := (Limbs => (others => 0));
   One  : constant Element := (Limbs => (0 => 1, 1 => 0));
   Reduction_Low : constant Unsigned_64 := 16#87#;

   subtype Bit_Index_128 is Natural range 0 .. 127;
   subtype Bit_Count     is Natural range 0 .. 128;
```

Aggregates `(others => 0)` and `(0 => 1, 1 => 0)` build arrays by index. A *subtype* is a type with a constraint; values of `Bit_Index_128` are integers that GNATprove knows lie in 0 to 127, which discharges the index checks inside `Bit`.

### 2.2 The spec: Bit, Add, Xtime

```ada
   function Bit (E : Element; I : Bit_Index_128) return Boolean is
     ((Shift_Right (E.Limbs (Limb_Index (I / 64)), I mod 64) and 1) = 1)
   with Ghost, Global => null;
```

An *expression function*: the body is a single expression written in the spec. GNATprove can unfold it wherever it is used, so contracts written with `Bit` are transparent to the prover. `Global => null` states that the function reads and writes no global variables; GNATprove checks it (flow analysis) and every subprogram in the package declares it. `Ghost` makes `Bit` part of the specification only: it can be called from contracts and from other ghost code, never from executable code, and the compiler drops it when assertions are disabled. That matters here because its body compiles to a branch on the bit read (a `tbz` instruction on AArch64), which the constant-time discipline of Section 2.4 forbids in executable code; as a ghost function it has no executable path. `Bit (E, I)` is the vocabulary of all later contracts: the coefficient of x^I.

```ada
   function Add (A, B : Element) return Element with
     Global => null,
     Post   =>
       (for all I in Limb_Index =>
          Add'Result.Limbs (I) = (A.Limbs (I) xor B.Limbs (I)));
```

`Post` is the postcondition: a Boolean expression over the parameters and `Add'Result` that GNATprove proves to hold on every return. `for all I in Limb_Index => ...` is a quantified expression, allowed in contracts. This postcondition is the complete definition of addition, so `Add` is Gold in the strongest sense: the contract is the specification.

```ada
   function Xtime (E : Element) return Element with
     Global => null,
     Post   =>
       Xtime'Result.Limbs (1) =
         (Shift_Left (E.Limbs (1), 1) or Shift_Right (E.Limbs (0), 63))
       and then
       Xtime'Result.Limbs (0) =
         (Shift_Left (E.Limbs (0), 1)
            xor (if Bit (E, 127) then Reduction_Low else 0));
```

Again the postcondition is the exact bit-level definition of Section 1.4: the high limb is shifted with bit 63 of the low limb coming in, the low limb is shifted and conditionally xored with `Reduction_Low`. The `if ... then ... else` inside the contract is an *if-expression*; it is allowed in contracts, and the implementation must achieve the same effect without a branch (Section 2.4).

### 2.3 The spec: Mul_Ref and Mul

```ada
   function Mul_Ref (A, B : Element; N : Bit_Count) return Element is
     (if N = 0 then
        Zero
      elsif Bit (B, 128 - N) then
        Add (Xtime (Mul_Ref (A, B, N - 1)), A)
      else
        Xtime (Mul_Ref (A, B, N - 1)))
   with
     Ghost,
     Global => null,
     Subprogram_Variant => (Decreases => N);
```

This is the reference of Section 1.5 written as a recursive expression function over the top N bits of B: `Mul_Ref (A, B, N)` is A times the polynomial formed by bits 127 down to 128 - N of B, reduced. `Ghost` means the function exists for specification and proof only: it can be called from contracts and ghost code, and it is compiled out when assertions are disabled. `Subprogram_Variant => (Decreases => N)` tells GNATprove which quantity strictly decreases on each recursive call, so termination is proved (a VC per recursive call).

```ada
   function Mul (A, B : Element) return Element with
     Global => null,
     Post   => Mul'Result = Mul_Ref (A, B, 128);
```

The postcondition of multiplication is "equal to the reference with all 128 bits consumed". This is the central Gold contract of the stage: GNATprove proves it for every pair of inputs ([ADR 0005](../adr/0005-mul-ref-as-proof-target.md)).

`Square` has `Post => Square'Result = Mul_Ref (E, E, 128)`, and `Inv` has no postcondition, only `Global => null`.

### 2.4 The body: Xtime without a branch

```ada
   function Xtime (E : Element) return Element is
      Carry : constant Unsigned_64 := Shift_Right (E.Limbs (1), 63);
      Mask  : constant Unsigned_64 := 0 - Carry;
   begin
      return
        (Limbs =>
           (0 => Shift_Left (E.Limbs (0), 1) xor (Mask and Reduction_Low),
            1 => Shift_Left (E.Limbs (1), 1)
                   or Shift_Right (E.Limbs (0), 63)));
   end Xtime;
```

`Carry` is 0 or 1. In a modular type, `0 - 1` wraps to all ones and `0 - 0` is zero, so `Mask` is a constant-time selector: `Mask and Reduction_Low` is `Reduction_Low` when the carry is set and zero otherwise, with no conditional jump. GNATprove proves that this equals the if-expression of the postcondition; that single VC is the whole proof of `Xtime`.

### 2.5 The body: Mul_Serial and its loop invariant

Until milestone M2 this was the body of `Mul`. It stays as `Mul_Serial`, with the same postcondition, as the reference that the harness compares the faster `Mul` of Section 2.7 with.

```ada
   function Mul (A, B : Element) return Element is
      R : Element := Zero;
      M : Unsigned_64;
   begin
      for K in Bit_Count range 1 .. 128 loop
         R := Xtime (R);
         M := 0 - (Shift_Right (B.Limbs (Limb_Index ((128 - K) / 64)),
                                (128 - K) mod 64)
                     and 1);
         R.Limbs (0) := R.Limbs (0) xor (M and A.Limbs (0));
         R.Limbs (1) := R.Limbs (1) xor (M and A.Limbs (1));
         pragma Loop_Invariant (R = Mul_Ref (A, B, K));
      end loop;
      return R;
   end Mul;
```

Iteration K consumes bit 128 - K of B, from 127 down to 0, exactly as `Mul_Ref` unfolds. The mask `M` selects A without a branch, as in `Xtime`.

`pragma Loop_Invariant` is the heart of the proof. GNATprove reasons about a loop by induction: it proves the invariant holds the first time it is reached (base case, K = 1: one `Xtime` of zero, then possibly A, which is what `Mul_Ref (A, B, 1)` says), and that if it holds at the end of iteration K then it holds at the end of iteration K + 1 (preservation: one more `Xtime` and conditional `Add`, matching one more unfolding of `Mul_Ref`). Inside the loop, the prover knows nothing about `R` except what the invariant says; after the loop it knows the invariant at the last value of K, which is `R = Mul_Ref (A, B, 128)`, literally the postcondition.

The invariant carries two properties at once, and it must carry both. First, the exact value of the accumulator: a weaker statement such as "R is some partial product" could not be preserved, because preservation needs to know what one more step does to the actual value. Second, the position in the computation: the K in `Mul_Ref (A, B, K)` ties the accumulator to the number of bits consumed, which is what makes the exit value coincide with the postcondition. Drop the first and preservation fails; drop the second and the exit lemma fails. Six verification conditions cover `Mul`: the invariant's base case and preservation, the index and shift-amount checks, and the postcondition.

### 2.6 The body: Square, Square_N and Inv

`Square` returns `Mul (E, E)`; its postcondition follows from the postcondition of `Mul` with both arguments equal, so the single VC is discharged by composition. `Square_N (E, N)` is a helper declared only in the body (invisible to callers), squaring N times in a `for` loop; it has no run-time checks to prove. `Inv` is a sequence of thirteen constant declarations, each one step of the chain of Section 1.8, and a final `Square`. There is no loop over exponent bits and no `if`: the only verification conditions are termination.

### 2.7 Since M2: the carry-less multiplication of upstream

`Mul` is now the software multiplication of upstream's `generic.rs` ([ADR 0065](../adr/0065-carry-less-multiplication-in-gf2-128.md)), seven times faster than the bit-by-bit form:

- each operand is split into its two limbs, and three 64 by 64 carry-less products are made (Karatsuba): low by low, high by high, and the xor of the limbs by the xor of the limbs;
- the 256-bit product is reduced by two folds, `Reduce`, each one using x^128 = x^7 + x^2 + x + 1 on one word.

The 64 by 64 products live in their own unit, `Clmul64`. The low word, `Clmul_Lo`, uses ordinary integer multiplication. Each operand is split into four classes of bit positions, one bit in four, and the sixteen products of one class by one class are added up, then masked. The high word, `Clmul_Hi`, is the low word of the bit-reversed operands, reversed and shifted right by one.

`Mul` calls `Clmul_Kara`, which makes the three products together and shares the reversals:

- each limb is reversed once, and the reversed middle operands are the xors of the reversed limbs;
- the high word of the middle term is reversed once, after the other two products are added in.

So the three high words cost seven reversals instead of nine. Its postcondition states the three products with `Lo_Ref` and `Hi_Ref`, as three calls of `Clmul_Lo` and `Clmul_Hi` would.

The postcondition of `Mul` is unchanged, `Mul'Result = Mul_Ref (A, B, 128)`, so no caller changes. The proof goes in three layers:

1. **The integer products.** `Clmul_Lo` equals `Lo_Ref (X, Y, 64)`, the xor of X shifted left over the bits of Y. A masked word is a number in base 16 whose digits are 0 or 1. In the integer product of two such words, the digit k collects at most k + 1 terms, so it stays below 16 except for the top digit, whose carry leaves the word. The low bit of each digit is therefore the parity of its terms, which is the carry-less coefficient. The lemmas follow a partial product over the bits of Y, with a bound on every digit, and show with 4-bit windows that adding one more term never carries into a bit that the mask keeps.
2. **The bit reversal.** `Clmul_Hi` equals `Hi_Ref (X, Y, 64)`, the high word of the same product, by the properties of the reversal: it swaps shifts left and right, and it distributes over xor. The same two properties prove `Clmul_Kara`.
3. **The field product.** Both `Mul_Ref` and the reduced Karatsuba product are linear in B. On a single bit j of B both give A times x^j: `Mul_Ref` by its definition, the Karatsuba product because it is A shifted by j, and its reduction agrees with `Xtime` step by step. By induction over the bits of B, they agree on every B.

Three lessons of this proof:

- An integer multiplication in a proof context slows down every step around it. The product is hidden behind a ghost function, `Prod`, which a single lemma opens.
- The quantified predicates of the induction (`Digits_Ok`, `Bits_Eq`) are hidden too, and opened only in small lemmas.
- The predefined `=` on `Element`, a record with an array, compares the limbs. The provers do not take it as the logical equality, so `Mul_Ref (A, B1) = Mul_Ref (A, B2)` from `B1 = B2` needs its own lemma, `Lemma_Mul_Ref_Ext`.

`Square` is still `Mul (E, E)` and gets faster with it.

## 3. What the proofs guarantee

GNATprove 16.1 with the project's prover configuration ([ADR 0020](../adr/0020-prover-configuration-step-budget.md): CVC5, Z3 and Alt-Ergo under a step budget), all 41 checks proved, no check above one second:

| Category | Checks | Discharged by |
|----------|--------|---------------|
| Data dependencies (`Global => null`) | 9 | flow analysis |
| Initialization | 1 | flow analysis |
| Run-time checks (indices, shift amounts, ranges) | 12 | provers |
| Assertions (loop invariant of `Mul`, variant of `Mul_Ref`) | 4 | provers |
| Functional contracts (`Add`, `Xtime`, `Mul`, `Square`) | 4 | provers |
| Termination | 11 | 9 flow, 2 provers |

Per subprogram: `Add` 6, `Bit` 3, `Mul` 6, `Mul_Ref` 5, `Square` 1, `Xtime` 1; `Inv`, `Square_N`, `Sub` and the package itself have no checks beyond flow and termination.

What this means, in plain terms:

- No run-time error can occur in the package for any input: no overflow, no index out of range, no uninitialised read. This holds for every one of the 2^256 pairs of operands, not for the tested ones.
- `Add` is addition, `Xtime` is multiplication by x with reduction, exactly as the bit-level postconditions state, for every input.
- `Mul (A, B) = Mul_Ref (A, B, 128)`, `Mul_Serial (A, B) = Mul_Ref (A, B, 128)` and `Square (E) = Mul_Ref (E, E, 128)` for every input. Since M2 this includes the integer products of `Clmul64`. If `Mul_Ref` is the field multiplication, then so are `Mul` and `Square`, with no exception anywhere in the input space.
- Every subprogram terminates.

Reproduce with `make prove`, which runs GNATprove with the switches of `package Prove` in the project file and prints the summary; the per-subprogram counts are in `obj/development/gnatprove/gnatprove.out`.

## 4. What is only tested

The proof relates `Mul` to `Mul_Ref`, and `Mul_Ref` to nothing. A wrong polynomial, a wrong reduction constant or a reflected bit order in `Mul_Ref` would have been proved just as happily. The known-answer vectors in `tests/vectors/` close this gap ([ADR 0009](../adr/0009-known-answer-vectors.md)); the harness ([ADR 0010](../adr/0010-assertion-enabled-test-project.md)) runs them with assertions enabled. Until the consolidation after Phase A every `Mul` also evaluated its postcondition at run time; since [ADR 0044](../adr/0044-field-contracts-at-proof-only-level.md) `Mul_Ref`, `Bit` and the postconditions that use them are at the proof-only assertion level, proved for all inputs and never compiled, and what the harness checks of `Mul` is its result against the vectors.

Why the upstream subfield basis is a genuinely external anchor: g = x^e depends on the polynomial, on the bit order and on the reduction constant all at once, and the sixteen values beta_i = g^i were computed by two upstream implementations (C++ and Rust) that agree with each other. The generator recomputes them with a reference written from scratch, with a different structure from `Mul_Ref` and from the upstream Rust (full 255-bit schoolbook product, then long division), and aborts on any mismatch. Agreement of three independent computations on sixteen 128-bit values is not a coincidence a bug could produce. The remaining anchors (x * x^-1 = 1, x^127 * x = 16#87#) are derived from the definition by hand.

| File | Cases | Anchors | What they establish |
|------|-------|---------|---------------------|
| `gf2_128_mul.txt` | 105 | 22 | `Mul_Ref` (hence `Mul`) is the draft's multiplication: beta_i * g = beta_(i+1), beta_i * beta_j = beta_(i+j), x * x^-1 = 1 |
| `gf2_128_square.txt` | 48 | 8 | beta_i^2 = beta_(2i); squaring is additive in the generator |
| `gf2_128_inv.txt` | 56 | 17 | `Inv (x) = x^-1`, `Inv (beta_i) = g^(65535 - i)`, `E * Inv (E) = 1`, `Inv (Zero) = Zero`; Fermat and Euclid references agree |

## 5. What stays trusted, and the limits

- That `Mul_Ref` defines multiplication is a mathematical statement (Horner's rule), stated in the header and anchored by vectors, not proved in SPARK.
- `Inv` is correct by Fermat's little theorem; SPARK proves only that it cannot fail and terminates. The vectors test the property on 56 elements, among them all sixteen basis elements, which is strong evidence and not a proof.
- Constant time is a discipline, scoped to assertion-free builds ([ADR 0006](../adr/0006-constant-time-discipline-and-scope.md)). Nothing in this chapter measures it, and the test binary is explicitly not constant time.
- The compiler, GNATprove, the SMT solver and the pinned draft are trusted ([ADR 0003](../adr/0003-proof-policy-and-trust-model.md)).
- Performance is not a goal of this stage: `Mul` is a 128-step software loop and `Inv` costs 139 of them. The Frobenius squaring and, later, hardware carry-less multiplication behind a proved interface are the planned optimisations.
